Assessing Spatial Equity in Multimodal Accessibility to Cooling-Effective Urban Green Space: A Case Study of London

Chapter 5 Discussion

Building on the results as established in Section 4, this section will evaluate the key findings (5.1 and 5.2) and implications of these results in the context of green infrastructure and urban heat planning (5.3) in response to our research question of to what extent is multimodal accessibility to cooling-effective UGS equitably distributed across heat exposed and socioeconomically deprived areas in London? Following, this section will also discuss transferability (5.4), limitations (5.5) and outline directions for future research (5.6) for this study.

5.1 Availability of Cooling-Effective Urban Green Space and Accessibility

Given the spatial disparities in surface heat exposure across London (Figure 9), the findings highlighted the potential role of UGS as cooling infrastructure, consistent with prior evidence (Norton et al., 2015). Although previous literature suggests that higher UGS coverage and fragmented spatial configurations can influence cooling performance and green space connectivity (Wang and Ren, 2025), such measures may overlook whether UGS demonstrate effective cooling and are publicly accessible. This is evident in the case of London, where cooling performances varied substantially between UGS, with only approximately one third (32.77%) of the UGS studied were identified as both cooling-effective and publicly accessible (Figure 10) as cooling infrastructure (defined in Section 2.2 and 2.3). In good agreement with previous studies (Xu et al., 2022), having a large supply of UGS distributed across London may not translate into having an equally extensive network of UGS as cooling infrastructure.

Despite Outer London’s wider provision of green infrastructure through the metropolitan Green Belt (Weihermüller and Doyle, 2026), this does not necessarily translate into reduced travel time to, or having more accessible opportunities for cooling-effective UGS for these surrounding neighbourhoods. This may reflect the geography of Outer London, which is generally characterised by weaker transport infrastructure and higher levels of car dependency (Lee and van Dijk, 2026). Moreover, the observed spatial patterns may also reflect London’s historical urban development, whereby earlier industrialisation contributed to a compact urban structure with higher density of population and transport provision within urban centres (Duan, Chen and Roosli, 2026).

As established in literature review, existing green space accessibility policy standards and studies often favour proximity and walking-based measures (Kabisch et al., 2016; Natural England, 2026a). However, as pointed by Geurs and van Wee (2004, p. 137), “interpretation can be much improved by estimating the separate influence of the different components of accessibility”. Acknowledging network-based travel time across different transport modes, our results demonstrated that modal disparities also shaped differentiated cooling-effective UGS opportunities across neighbourhoods in London, generally consistent with previous studies (S. Li et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). We found that cycling provided stronger local accessibility within 15 minutes, whereas PT enabled access to a substantially wider scope of opportunities reachable within 60 minutes. This highlights that beyond UGS provision, the relative accessibility of neighbourhoods varies depending on how accessibility is measured. In the context of London, simple “closeness” and a focus on walking access may therefore neglect actual travel conditions and oversimplify how the transport network shapes cooling-effective UGS access in reality. Nevertheless, these findings indicate potential accessibility, as real-world accessibility also depends on one’s need, ability, and willingness to travel.

5.2 Spatial Equity in Cooling-Effective Urban Green Space Accessibility

The findings from BYM2 models showed limited evidence of consistent independent relationships between socioeconomic deprivation and poorer cooling-effective UGS accessibility across most models. Although this contrasts with common theories of environmental and distributional injustice in previous research, which often conceptualise disadvantaged populations as having lower UGS access (Boone and Klinsky, 2015), it is compatible with other studies where socioeconomic inequalities can be inconsistent (Rigolon, 2016). In the UK context, one possible explanation may relate to the historical legacy of UGS distribution, where earlier placements of working-class neighbourhoods near UGS as a public health measure may have contributed to differentiated access in certain deprived neighbourhoods to date (Mears et al., 2019).

Notably, our results indicate higher heat exposure was associated with higher accessibility across models. This can partly be explained by London’s urban structure, whereby denser urban areas may retain greater heat while also providing greater connectivity to wider cooling opportunities. Meanwhile, socioeconomic deprivation appears to modify the relationship between higher heat exposure and accessibility. As established in Section 2.3, cooling justice advocates for equitable access in relation to heat exposure and socioeconomic deprivation. From a distributional EJ perspective, London’s evidence showed some misalignment between cooling-effective UGS accessibility and environmental and socioeconomic needs, broadly consistent with the mixed demand-oriented equity findings across international evidence (Tian et al., 2024). This therefore uncovers relative disadvantage for socioeconomically deprived neighbourhoods but does not illustrate an overall distinct pattern of inequality across London.

Our BiLISA spatial clustering analysis usefully complemented the BYM2 models, revealing the spatial heterogeneity at the local level, agreeing that inequity “is more evident at smaller spatial scales” (Hu et al., 2020, p. 7). Looking into the L-H clusters, our findings highlighted spatial mismatch with neighbourhoods with poorer cooling-effective UGS access and surrounding higher heat exposure or higher socioeconomic deprivation as a relative inequity condition to be concerned for. Such patterns indicated that access to cooling opportunities does not necessarily correspond with areas of relatively greater need in the case of London, broadly similar to earlier findings by Wang, Ouyang and Zhang (2024). These L-H clusters also coincide in certain neighbourhoods (see Figure 13, Figure 14), showing neighbourhoods with compounded cooling justice concerns. These disparities may reflect London’s land use pressures, where conventional green-space planning tends to be constrained by a trade-off between protecting and enhancing UGS while addressing affordable housing needs (Whitten, 2022). Such densification may exacerbate urban heat through increased impervious surface cover, thereby intensifying existing cooling inequities (Norton et al., 2015). From the cooling justice perspective, identifying these potential spatial mismatches as priority “hotspots” allows more targeted local interventions according to relative community needs.

Regardless, the L-H clusters persist and vary across modes and under different travel time thresholds. This adds on our discussion in Section 5.1, that examining through a single-mode measure may not provide a comprehensive picture of cooling-effective UGS accessibility inequity, as people from different socioeconomic groups may adopt different travel behaviours and mode choices (Hu et al., 2020). Taking everything into account, cooling-effective UGS accessibility does not show a global equity pattern but instead persists through localised spatial mismatches. As such, these findings highlight key areas of prioritisation, of which their implications will be further discussed in Section 5.3.

5.3 Implications for Urban Heat Policy

The L-H clusters presented and discussed in Section 4.3 and 5.2 warrant attention, pointing to where green infrastructure interventions could be prioritised and targeted alongside broader green infrastructure network planning across London. Notably, several boroughs in London would benefit from priority policy interventions, namely areas within Hillingdon, Ealing, Hounslow, Enfield, Haringey, Havering and Barking and Dagenham, where L-H clusters were persistent across travel modes and time thresholds. This aligns with the recent publication of Heat Ready London, which stresses the need to “reduce inequalities by prioritising those most at risk … impacted by heat” and “with the least access to green space” as key objectives for achieving a heat-resilient London (Mayor of London, 2026b, pp. 9–10, 21).

Further, the recently released Draft London Plan 2026 demonstrates growing attention to urban heat within London’s planning agenda. While the London Plan 2021 recognises the need to manage heat risk (SI4), the current draft shows growing policy emphasis on heat risk (GHR4) and improving access to green space (GHR8), particularly in areas of deficiency (AoD) and local green infrastructure priorities (Mayor of London, 2026a). While AoD provides a useful policy benchmark, its proximity-based nature may overlook differences in access across modes. Based on our neighbourhood-level multimodal accessibility findings discussed previously, this provides complementary insights into where and through which transport modes access to existing cooling opportunities is relatively limited, allowing more targeted accessibility improvements to existing provision. As acquiring new UGS is complicated and expensive (Rigolon, 2016), improving access to existing cooling infrastructure may offer a more achievable short-term approach for London for reducing current accessibility gaps. Furthermore, given that the Draft London Plan is currently under public consultation, such evidence could also contribute to identifying areas of local green infrastructure accessibility deficiencies and inform heat-resilience interventions.

Beyond the shortlisted areas of priority intervention, improving awareness of the availability and accessibility of these cooling opportunities is crucial, given EJ is also a “fairness in process” (Boone and Klinsky, 2015, p. 330). London’s current policy tool, the Cool Space Map recognises indoor and outdoor spaces for heat mitigation during hot weather, yet limits outdoor cooling opportunities features to tree canopy cover and vaguely “cooler area” only (Mayor of London, 2024a). Following our identification of cooling-effective UGS across London in Section 5.1, such evidence could be integrated into existing policy mapping tools to further promote outdoor cooling opportunities and reinforce the role of UGS to facilitate as cooling infrastructure for heat mitigation.

More importantly, to realise the full potential of UGS as cooling infrastructure also depends on managing and maintaining their capacity for the long-term. Following recent drought conditions and wildfire events across London, this highlighted the vulnerability of green spaces under prolonged periods of extreme heat. It is therefore important to ensure the quality and safety of these spaces alongside appropriate vegetation management to sustain their cooling benefits and ensure usability during high temperatures.

5.4 Transferability

While previous sections discussed the implications for the case of London, the reproducible methodological framework implemented in this study could also be applied to other metropolitan regions in the UK (e.g. Greater Manchester) or globally. Even so, the full replicability is subject to data availability in the selected study context, and accessibility modelling parameters and thresholds may require adjustments according to the specific urban context. More importantly, London-specific findings should not be directly extrapolated to other contexts with different urban structure and sociodemographic geographies, as such variations may shape how the outcomes should be understood.

5.5 Limitations

Several data and methodological limitations that may influence the temporal and spatial interpretation of our findings is recognised. Firstly, there is a temporal mismatch across datasets (2021-2026) due to data availability. Covering only one summer period, our findings therefore represent a cross-sectional assessment encompassing different time periods, where LST variations between years or extreme heat events fall beyond the study scope.

Methodologically, while excluding UGS < 2ha is informed by wider literature and policy benchmarks, this may potentially overlook the cooling benefits of smaller UGS (i.e. pocket parks), particularly in urban centres where smaller UGS contribute to a substantial proportion of local UGS provision. Additionally, disturbances such as vegetation, water bodies and large impervious surfaces within buffer rings were not removed. The calculated mean LST of surrounding areas may reflect influences of the wider environmental context on top of the cooling effect of the UGS. It is also worth noting that satellite-derived LST represents surface thermal conditions (as discussed in Section 2.4). Accordingly, the identified cooling effects should be interpreted as surface cooling, not a direct representation human thermal comfort.

This study also recognises the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP), whereby aggregating data under different spatial scales can provide inconsistent results (Wong, 2009). Using LSOA as spatial units for analyses although is considered a practical choice here, it may compromise interpretation of data, introduce statistical bias and overlook accessibility variations within certain neighbourhoods. As our BYM2 spatial effects were defined by LSOA-level Queen contiguity spatial weights, the remaining residuals spatial autocorrelation and predictive miscalibration in our models may partially reflect sensitivity to the specified spatial structure. Nevertheless, the models still provided inferential conclusions regarding the observed associations.

Despite the shortcomings, our findings provide a preliminary neighbourhood-scale assessment of equity in potential accessibility to cooling-effective UGS across London, highlighting new avenues for future research and applications.

5.6 Future Direction

Following Section 5.5, future directions could extend on these three areas. 1) Future research can expand on the temporal scope across longer periods and/or multiple seasons for a more comprehensive understanding of the temporal stability of UGS cooling performances and spatial mismatches. 2) Further accounting for UGS capacity, quality (e.g. attractiveness, safety), and population perceptions can complement objective accessibility measures, allowing a more comprehensive assessment of cooling justice. Improving reachable opportunities is important, yet they may overlook how UGS are perceived or used by local communities, as residents in some cases “do not see the greenspaces as provided for them” (Jones, Hillsdon and Coombes, 2009, p. 5). 3) Moving beyond the identified L-H clusters, further research can examine which urban environmental characteristics are associated with these mismatches at local levels, and identify context specific interventions for improving cooling accessibility (Zeng et al., 2026).
