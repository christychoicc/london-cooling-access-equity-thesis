Assessing Spatial Equity in Multimodal Accessibility to Cooling-Effective Urban Green Space: A Case Study of London

Chapter 2 Literature Review

As established in the introduction, urban heat is a growing environmental, public health and socio-spatial challenge. While UGS has been identified as a mitigation strategy, its benefits are unevenly distributed and may not be accessible to those most exposed or vulnerable. Building on this, this section is structured into four parts: the problem and policy context of urban heat (2.1), the role of UGS as a cooling infrastructure for mitigating urban heat (2.2), accessibility to cooling-effective UGS as an environmental justice (EJ) concern (2.3), and methodological approaches for studying cooling accessibility and spatial equity (2.4).

2.1 Urban Heat Problem

Urban heat is one of the major climate risks faced by urban populations, widely recognised as an urban hazard resulting from the combined effects of climate change and urbanisation, with significant implications for urban sustainability and liveability (Mohajerani, Bakaric and Jeffrey-Bailey, 2017). As stated in the introduction, urban heat challenges wider global goals for sustainable and equitable development, as its impacts span beyond environmental concerns to public health and social inequality. Commonly conceptualised through the UHI effect, it reflects complex interactions between urban morphology, land cover and surface energy balance (Yeboah et al., 2025). The replacement of permeable surfaces with dense built materials (i.e. asphalt, concrete) increases heat absorption and storage while reducing evaporative cooling, thereby intensifying the UHI effect (Balany et al., 2020; Yeboah et al., 2025). However, urban heat is spatially uneven and varies significantly across the urban landscape (Moffett, Makido and Shandas, 2019). Variations in land surface materials, spatial distribution of buildings and building geometry can collectively influence local thermal conditions at the neighbourhood level (Kleerekoper, van Esch and Salcedo, 2012; Chen et al., 2016; Heaviside, Macintyre and Vardoulakis, 2017). In light of such intra-urban variation, urban heat should not be perceived as a spatially consistent phenomenon, but a spatially differentiated pattern of heat exposure.

2.1.1 Vulnerability of Cities to Urban Heat

Building upon the intra-urban variation in urban heat, while land surface temperature (LST) can identify where thermal exposure is concentrated, it only provides a partial indication of urban heat, not fully explaining what populations are most at risk. (Karanja and Kiage, 2021; Niu et al., 2021). This is important as areas experiencing high temperatures do not necessarily correspond to areas of greater vulnerability. In this sense, urban heat is a socio-spatial problem, shaped by the interaction between physical heat exposure, social characteristics and sensitivity, building structure, housing density and access to resources (Brenkert and Malone, 2005; Wilhelmi and Hayden, 2010; Leal Filho et al., 2018; Karanja and Kiage, 2021; Li et al., 2022). Conceptually, literature emphasises that heat risk emerges through the intersection of hazard, exposure and vulnerability (Cardona et al., 2012). The IPCC (2001, p. 16) defines vulnerability as “degree to which a system is susceptible to or unable to cope with, adverse effects of climate change, including climate variability and extremes”, while Cutter (2006) specifies this as an individual potential for harm shaped by both spatial and non-spatial domains. Under this context, the impacts of high temperatures are mediated not only by where and when heat occurs, but also by who is exposed, who is more sensitive and who has the adaptive capacity to respond and cope (Kolokotroni and Giridharan, 2008; Karanja and Kiage, 2021; Li et al., 2022).

The complexity of vulnerability is recognised in literature, often approached through topics of physiological sensitivity and wider societal conditions (Alonso and Renard, 2020). Physiological sensitivity refers to the physical risk factors that shape individuals’ response to heat. Extreme heat can exacerbate existing health conditions and increase heat-related hospitalisation, morbidity and mortality, especially during severe heatwave events (Heaviside, Macintyre and Vardoulakis, 2017). For instance, Hadei et al. (2024) found a 23% increase in heat-related mortality rate during heatwave events compared with normal days. This is particularly damaging for individuals with pre-existing physical and mental health conditions, older and younger populations, as these groups are more vulnerable due to reduced thermoregulation capacity and greater susceptibility to heat stress (Heaviside, Macintyre and Vardoulakis, 2017; Balany et al., 2020; Niu et al., 2021).

Beyond physical exposure, the consequences of urban heat are also shaped by social vulnerability, with factors such as socioeconomic status, neighbourhood context, and access to resources influencing how individuals and communities are exposed to, manage and respond to heat (Niu et al., 2021). Mitchell and Chakraborty (2015) describe this as a “climate gap”, identifying significant associations between urban heat risk factors and lower socioeconomic status. Such inequalities may reflect prior planning, housing, and disinvestment practices that contributed to reduced green space provision, higher levels of heat exposure and limited access to cooling resources in marginalised communities (Xiao et al., 2023; Huang, Dombrowski and Stucky, 2025). Likewise, Niu et al. (2021) and Chen (2024) argue that heat disparities occur across certain population and income groups, who may face greater exposure to adverse heat impacts and lower adaptive capacity.

In urban contexts, adaptive capacity is influenced by whether there is access to cooling resources. Studies have found that poorer neighbourhoods are disproportionately exposed to extreme heat and often have fewer physical and social resources (Harlan et al., 2006; Heaviside, Macintyre and Vardoulakis, 2017; Xie et al., 2026). Typically, higher surface temperatures are commonly associated with areas characterised by poverty, ethnic minority groups, lower educational attainment, older populations and higher crime rates (Heaviside, Macintyre and Vardoulakis, 2017), which may also have lower vegetation cover and reduced cooling capacity. Nevertheless, Alonso and Renard (2020) argue that physiological and socio-economic vulnerabilities vary spatially, with the former concentrating in urban centres and the latter in the periphery in Lyon. Specifically, socio-economic disadvantages and physiologically heat-sensitive populations do not necessarily concentrate in the same areas. This highlights that heat vulnerability should not be understood through physical proximity to heat, but through the interconnection between social, economic and biophysical conditions across multiple scales (Wilhelmi and Hayden, 2010). However, existing studies often measure heat vulnerability with different approaches, findings are not always comparable across different contexts. Therefore, addressing heat vulnerability requires a broader, context-specific socio-spatial approach that considers not only where heat occurs, but also whether communities can access the resources needed to cope.

Such socio-spatial nature of urban heat vulnerability is particularly relevant in London, where heat exposure, housing conditions, and social disadvantage intersect unevenly across neighbourhoods. Characterised by dense urban form, London has extensive heat-retaining surfaces that intensify local thermal conditions and contribute to spatial clustering in heat vulnerability, with discernible heat-related health impacts (Wolf and McGregor, 2013). Zooming into neighbourhood-level, survey conducted during the August 2025 heatwave found lived experiences of urban heat are unevenly distributed, with Londoners in social housing and those facing economic disadvantage reporting more severe heat discomfort, having “fewer cooling measures” at home (Hewlett et al., 2026, p. 5). More importantly, the same survey also found majority of respondents in London ranked nature-based solutions of green infrastructure as their top priority of action during very hot weather, with 84% recognised green spaces as providing potential cooling benefits (Hewlett et al., 2026). Given that, London’s urban heat problem is not only defined by where heat concentrates, but also by whether heat exposed and socially deprived neighbourhoods can realistically access spaces for thermal relief.

2.1.2 Global and Local Policies

Policies at both global and local scales have increasingly recognised urban heat as an equity and access problem. The United Nations (2023) emphasised the importance of making cities “inclusive, safe, resilient and sustainable” through the Sustainable Development Goal 11. While not specifically foregrounded within the SDGs, the UNEP’s Beating the Heat: A Sustainable Cooling Handbook For Cities identifies community-centric cooling as a way “to advance social equity and environmental justice” and emphasises that cooling benefits should be reachable by marginalised communities, with priority for “those with the highest need first” (UNEP, 2021, pp. 150, 151).

However, the effectiveness of these global initiatives depends on whether they are translated into local policy and planning actions. Existing urban heat-related policies have often focused on short-term management, including heat warning alerts, promoting behaviour change, and emergency service preparations (Norton et al., 2015). While these measures are important for reducing immediate heat risks, they do not fully address the structural and spatial causes of urban heat exposure. This also raises a temporal challenge, where policy response often lags accelerating climate risks. In the UK, the Climate Change Committee (2026) argues that “adaptation action is not keeping pace with increasing level of risk”. This also applies to England, where heat-related policies remain limited by insufficient understanding of who is vulnerable to extreme heat, how vulnerability varies spatially, and where neighbourhood-scale interventions should be targeted (Howarth et al., 2023).

This is also the case for London. The city demonstrates growing interest in mitigating heat risk as well as recognising the role of green infrastructure in responding to urban heat risk and environmental inequalities. This is evident through the London Plan 2021 (Mayor of London, 2021), setting out a spatial development strategy encompassing heat risk, green infrastructure and open space accessibility. Further policy frameworks and tools include the London Green Infrastructure Framework (Mayor of London, 2026c), heat risk mapping in the London Climate Risk Map (Mayor of London, 2024b), Cool Spaces Map (Mayor of London, 2024a) and Heat Ready London (Mayor of London, 2026b). Nevertheless, despite policy ambitions, London’s approach remains limited and fragmented (see Section 5.3), particularly in terms of linking cooling benefits of green infrastructure with accessibility during heat (Turcu, 2025). Furthermore, while strategic policies are often set at the municipal level, implementation relies on borough and local-level planning as well as cross-borough and cross-sector coordination. Given this implementation gap, this shows a need for London’s urban heat-related policy to move beyond recognition of green infrastructure and towards neighbourhood-scale evidence on whether cooling-effective spaces are accessible for heat-vulnerable communities.

2.2 Cooling Benefits of Urban Green Space

As discussed, studies and policies have emphasised UGS as an integrated climate adaptation strategy to counteract UHI effects and heat injustice. Increasingly positioned as a nature-based cooling infrastructure, UGS is widely recognised for its ability to regulate local thermal conditions and provision of public spaces for thermal relief (Bowler et al., 2010b; Norton et al., 2015). Vegetation has been proven to considerably intercept solar radiation and reduce heat absorbed and retained by land surfaces through shading, evapotranspiration, transpiration and thermal insulation (Bowler et al., 2010b; Kumar et al., 2024). For instance, parks are on average found to be 0.94 °C cooler, while specifically a study in London found an average cooling effect of 1.1°C and can reach up to 4°C at night time (Bowler et al., 2010b; Doick, Peace and Hutchings, 2014). Given this, UGS forms the park cool island effect, which creates cooler microclimatic conditions within green spaces than in adjacent built-up environments (Bowler et al., 2010b; Ren et al., 2013), providing higher levels of thermal comfort within otherwise heat-exposed urban environments (Aram et al., 2019; Balany et al., 2020).

However, the cooling effects of UGS are not uniform and do not provide equal levels of thermal relief. The extent of UGS’s cooling benefits depends on size, spatial configuration of the greenspace, also the form and structure of the surrounding urban environment, and the prevailing meteorological patterns (Doick, Peace and Hutchings, 2014; L. Li et al., 2025; Wang and Ren, 2025). Size is often identified as a key factor in cooling performance, as larger UGS is often associated with more significant cooling effects from a more diverse and extensive vegetation cover, providing greater cooling intensity and distance compared to smaller UGS (Aram et al., 2019; Peng et al., 2021). However, this should not be interpreted as “bigger is better” in cooling means. For instance, Peng et al. (2021) identified diminishing cooling returns beyond very large sized parks in Shenzhen, with an upper threshold of 429ha while Wang et al. (2025) suggested a much smaller optimal park area upper limit of 56ha in Hangzhou, where an increase in size will not significantly enhance the cooling effects. At the other end of the size scale, some studies suggest that pocket parks below 2ha may be too small to generate substantial cooling effects, whereas others shows that those as small as 0.2ha, can still contribute to a cooling of around 0.3 °C (Vaz Monteiro et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2025).

Additionally, spatial configuration also influences the magnitude and spatial extent of UGS cooling performance. Existing research commonly uses indicators of “cooling intensity” and “maximum cooling distance” to quantify cooling-effectiveness under different contexts, with cooling intensity measuring the temperature difference between the UGS and its surrounding environment and cooling distance measuring how far this cooling effect can extend (Wang et al., 2025). While compact green spaces are often found associated with stronger cooling intensity, irregular, fragmented and more complex shapes may extend cooling effects over longer distances through greater contact with the surrounding urban landscape (Feyisa, Dons and Meilby, 2014; Peng et al., 2021; L. Li et al., 2025). However, a study in London suggests that such greater edge effects may also come at the expense of lower cooling intensity, as higher complexity and perimeter/ area ratios may provide weaker internal cooling effects (Vaz Monteiro et al., 2016). These findings suggest that there is no “optimal” green space composition and configuration, as different spatial forms may influence the extent of cooling benefits differently (Peng et al., 2021). For this reason, park cooling intensity (PCI) indicators are often used to quantify the cooling effects of green spaces (Wu et al., 2025). Given this, whether UGS provides meaningful cooling benefits should be assessed spatially, considering the spatial continuity of cooling effects with increasing distance from UGS (Peng et al., 2021). In this sense, UGS should not be understood as a homogeneous form of cooling infrastructure, as UGS provision does not directly mean cooling-effectiveness. Therefore, it is necessary to identify and distinguish green spaces that demonstrate measurable cooling benefits as cooling infrastructure.

2.3 Equitable Access to Cooling Infrastructure

Building on Section 2.2, in the context of urban heat, cooling benefits of UGS should not be evaluated only by their potential cooling performance, but also whether they are accessible. Accessibility here refers to the availability and “ease” of reaching UGS and its provided qualities by “overcoming spatial barriers” of distance, time and cost associated with access to diverse means of transport modes (Liu, Kwan and Kan, 2021; Huang et al., 2023; Czesak and Różycka-Czas, 2025). Existing studies often measure UGS accessibility through proximity and threshold-based approaches, such as static Euclidean distance, distance decay approaches, buffer-based standards of 300-500m, and increasingly the 15-minute city concept (Kabisch et al., 2016; Jalkanen et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2023; Almeida and Fonseca, 2025; Czesak and Różycka-Czas, 2025). Similar standards are applied in the UK, where the Accessible Greenspace Standard recognises local accessibility as having access to at least 2ha of greenspace within 300m from home (Natural England, 2026a). Although these measures and policy tools provide simple, comparable and policy relevant benchmarks, they remain limited as accessibility is multidimensional, primarily considering proximity may overlook other features such as quality (Rigolon et al., 2018). While quality often refers to amenities, maintenance and safety across literature, cooling-effectiveness represents a functional quality of UGS that is essential for these benefits to be realised under urban heat contexts (Rigolon et al., 2018; Liang et al., 2023). Accessibility should therefore not only be understood as the spatial presence of UGS within a given distance, but as realistic access to cooling-effective UGS as cooling infrastructure.

Given the complexity of real-world travel patterns, accessibility to UGS is also shaped by transport system attributes, temporal variability and individual spatiotemporal constraints (Nie et al., 2024; Schendl et al., 2025). Infrastructure-based metrics are often used to provide a more comprehensive indication of how individuals practically move through cities, incorporating distance to public transport stops, travel times, network connectivity and service levels (Nie et al., 2024). Additionally, it is widely acknowledged that individuals experience different levels of accessibility, as they may adopt different travel modes (i.e. walking, cycling and public transit (PT)) even when travelling to the same destinations, depending on the relationship between distance, travel time and willingness to travel (Chen et al., 2022). For instance, studies show that individuals often use public transport to access larger UGS, and that frequently visited UGS may be located well beyond commonly assumed travel-distance thresholds (Czesak and Różycka-Czas, 2025; Hassan et al., 2025). This is particularly relevant in large cities with dense public transport and pedestrian infrastructure like London, where simple thresholds can be misleading as urban centres may benefit more from short-distance access, while outer areas depend more on rail networks (Duan, Chen and Roosli, 2026). Travel-time based measures are therefore considered more realistic and appropriate as they account for transport mode, network structure and temporal variation by time of day and day of the week (Miller, 2018). Given this, evaluating accessibility under multimodal transportation can capture heterogeneous accessibility patterns, enabling mode- and location-specific disparities to cooling-effective UGS to be identified and examined across neighbourhoods.

Ensuring adequate and equitable access to cooling infrastructure has become an increasing EJ concern in the context of urban heat and as a critical aspect of sustainable development (Xu et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2024). By definition, EJ refers to the unequal exposure to environmental burdens and the unequal access to environmental goods experienced by different socioeconomic and ethnic groups, particularly on UGS (Rigolon, 2016; Kronenberg et al., 2020). As discussed in Section 2.1.2, UGS is often promoted as an urban heat mitigation strategy with institutional frameworks emphasising inclusivity and equity. Nevertheless, scholars documented cooling-effective UGS as a “luxury good” in reality, whereby such spaces are unevenly distributed, disproportionately concentrated in wealthier areas and less accessible from lower-income neighbourhoods (Y. Li et al., 2024; Ngan et al., 2025). On the other hand, this does not always systematically disadvantage lower socioeconomic groups, nor consistently favour those advantaged socioeconomic groups, as patterns of spatial equity vary by specific urban context and are sensitive to how accessibility is measured (Lu et al., 2024). Therefore, evaluating access should also consider the needs of different population groups (Williams et al., 2020). Building on Dong et al. (2024) and Williams et al. (2020), areas with higher heat risk or more vulnerable residents can be understood as having higher cooling needs, as they in many cases need and have most to gain from the benefits of cooling infrastructures. For this reason, this study extends the concept of EJ into a cooling justice problem, whereby inequalities are shaped by the interaction between heat exposure, physiological, social vulnerability (Section 2.1), and accessibility (Section 2.3) to cooling-effective UGS (Section 2.2). Specifically, these are examined through heat exposure and socioeconomic deprivation.

2.4 Measuring Cooling Accessibility and Spatial Equity

Having established access to cooling-effective UGS as a cooling justice concern in the previous sections, the following will review existing methodological approaches and inform methodology choices for this study. Existing literature employs a diverse approach to evaluate the UHI and cooling effect, using air temperature (Doick, Peace and Hutchings, 2014), LST (Xu et al., 2022; Krenz and Amann, 2025) or both (Du et al., 2024). Air temperature from in-situ measurements (i.e. data loggers,measurements from meteorological stations) is often considered to be a more representative measure of human thermal exposure (Doick, Peace and Hutchings, 2014; Y. Li et al., 2025). However, its spatial coverage is often limited by sparse distribution of measuring stations, resulting in low spatial resolution (Sobrino et al., 2012; Y. Li et al., 2025). Alternatively, thermal infrared remote sensing (RS) imagery is widely used to retrieve LST (Du et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023), providing spatially continuous measurements, suitable for city-scale analysis (Weng, 2009; Yang, Yan and Zhang, 2020; Li et al., 2023). Earlier studies relied on LST measurements by AVHRR and MODIS satellite sensors, recent research has increasingly used higher resolution ASTER (90m) and Landsat (30m) for capturing finer LST spatial variations (Imran et al., 2022). Nevertheless, satellite-derived LST is influenced by the sensor’s resolution, and may overestimate cooling benefits relative to ground level air temperature (Sobrino et al., 2012; Du et al., 2024). However, given the statistically significant relationship between LST and air temperature, LST is supported as a spatially comprehensive approach for identifying urban surface heating patterns at city-scale (Yang, Yan and Zhang, 2020; Krenz and Amann, 2025).

Moving on to cooling accessibility, simple distance-based measures may oversimplify true representations of urban mobilities. Given this, travel-time inputs estimated by assigning average travel speeds to road segments may not fully account for differences in routing between travel modes, potentially resulting in deviations in accessibility estimates (Chen et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2023). Advancing on this, studies attempted to use network-based approaches to improve the reliability of travel-time estimation through scheduled timetable feed data using a General Transit Feed Specification (GTFS) format (Pereira, Andrade and Vieira, 2023). GTFS provides detailed information on routes, timetables and stops, enabling more granular and scalable analyses for public transit accessibility (Pereira, Andrade and Vieira, 2023; Lee and van Dijk, 2026). To integrate GTFS data with road networks, open-source routing tools such as r5r have emerged as more transparent and reproducible alternatives to proprietary routing methods, enabling efficient multimodal computation of many-to-many pairs of origin-destination queries (Pereira et al., 2021). While previous studies have applied r5r to broader accessibility analysis, including public transport accessibility in the UK (Verduzco Torres and McArthur, 2024), spatial equity in job accessibility in London (Lee and van Dijk, 2026) and UGS accessibility across European cities (Wozniak, Radzimski and Wajchman-Świtalska, 2025), its applications to UGS with demonstrated cooling benefits remain limited.

Further, accessibility can be assessed over a variety of measures, including infrastructure-, location-, person- and utility-based measures (Geurs and van Wee, 2004; Miller, 2018). As “there is no objective, normative standard for what constitutes ‘good’ or ‘acceptable’ accessibility” (Miller, 2018, p. 553), measure selection should be tailored to the specific study purpose. For assessing cooling-effective UGS accessibility, location-based approaches are considered appropriate. Commonly used measures are summarised in Table 1, among which cumulative opportunity measures are considered suitable for quantifying the number of reachable opportunities.

While travel-time-based accessibility measures can reveal whether neighbourhoods are able to reach cooling-effective UGS, they are insufficient alone for determining whether access is shaped by underlying social and spatial inequalities. Across accessibility literature, inequalities have been commonly examined through descriptive, correlational, and mapping approaches, with regression frequently used to assess relationships between accessibility and socioeconomic or spatial factors (Hidayati, Tan and Yamu, 2021). While ordinary least squares (OLS) modelling has been applied to green space and cooling UGS accessibility equity studies (Wu et al., 2019; Lan et al., 2022), it is considered less suitable for this study given the count nature of cumulative opportunity measures and potential spatial dependence between neighbouring areas. Considering this, Bayesian hierarchical models (BHM) provide an alternative approach. Despite being widely used in disease mapping (Gause et al., 2024), BHM have also been applied to housing, health and well-being (Kandt et al., 2017) and UGS and health inequalities research (Liu et al., 2026), demonstrating their potential in examining spatial inequalities across urban contexts. Yet, global modelling approaches may hide spatially concentrated inequalities, which is crucial for identifying priority locations that could benefit from EJ-related interventions. Given this, local indicators of spatial association (LISA) can further identify any localised spatial mismatches. Studies also utilise a bivariate measure of LISA, so-called BiLISA, to further unveil mismatches between two geographical variables (Wang, Ouyang and Zhang, 2024). In this vein, this methodology allows moving beyond interpreting accessibility patterns to examining whether neighbourhoods with relatively greater needs systematically experience differentiated levels of cooling access.

Altogether, prior literature established urban heat as an uneven socio-spatial problem and UGS as a potential mitigation resource, less attention has been paid to whether UGS that provide measurable cooling are reachable by neighbourhoods with the greatest needs. To the best of our knowledge, no research in London has integrated UGS cooling performance, accessibility and heat vulnerability within one framework. Addressing this gap, this study integrates Earth observations, cooling effect assessments, travel-time accessibility and equity analyses to examine the extent to which heat exposed, and socioeconomically deprived neighbourhoods have equitable access to cooling-effective UGS. Adopting a vertical equity approach, greater equity is assumed when cooling-effective UGS opportunities increase with heat exposure and socioeconomic deprived neighbourhoods (Khanyile and Fatti, 2022). Likewise, low accessibility in areas of relatively higher need is therefore interpreted to be an EJ mismatch. Using London as a case study, the following sections provide neighbourhood-scale evidence to inform more targeted urban heat adaptation, supporting the city’s transition to becoming “heat ready”.
