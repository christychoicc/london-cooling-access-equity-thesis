Assessing Spatial Equity in Multimodal Accessibility to Cooling-Effective Urban Green Space: A Case Study of London

Chapter 1 Introduction

Extreme heat has emerged as one of the greatest challenges faced by cities around the world, driven by the intensification of rapid urbanisation and climate change. In response to these challenges, international policy frameworks such as the United Nations 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development and the New Urban Agenda emphasised the need for sustainable, inclusive and equitable solutions (United Nations, 2015; UN Habitat, 2020). Rising temperatures and more frequent extreme heat events place increasing pressure on urban habitability, resilience, and public services (Mohajerani, Bakaric and Jeffrey-Bailey, 2017). Cities are particularly vulnerable to the rapid expansion of built-up environments, often replacing natural land surfaces with impervious, heat-absorbent materials. This alters local microclimates, accelerates urban overheating and intensifies the urban heat island (UHI) effect, whereby urban air and surface temperatures are significantly higher than those in surrounding rural or peri-urban environments (Kleerekoper, van Esch and Salcedo, 2012; Mohajerani, Bakaric and Jeffrey-Bailey, 2017). More importantly, the consequence of urban overheating extends beyond environmental concerns to social and public health impacts. Exposure to elevated temperatures is associated with a range of adverse physical and mental health outcomes such as thermoregulatory stress, heat fatigue, heat stroke, and increased fatalities (Kleerekoper, van Esch and Salcedo, 2012; Heaviside, Macintyre and Vardoulakis, 2017; Xu et al., 2022). Yet, such impacts are not limited to the most extreme heat events. For instance, in England, an additional 1,311 heat-associated fatalities were recorded during summer heat episodes in 2024 despite being the coolest summer since 2015 (GOV.UK, 2026). This suggests that even under comparably less extreme conditions, urban heat can still place serious pressures on public health. As unprecedented extreme heat events become more frequent, prolonged, and intense (World Health Organization, 2026), urban heat is recognised not only as an environmental issue, but also increasingly a public health concern.

Urban green space (UGS) is widely recognised as a nature-based approach for mitigating urban heat and improving human health (Bowler et al., 2010a; Lin and Li, 2025). Urban vegetation can disrupt the thermal continuity of urban environments through shading, heat absorption, and evapotranspiration (Xu et al., 2022; Y. Li et al., 2024). Through the park cool island effect, UGS can reduce temperatures within green spaces and extend cooling benefits into surrounding areas, helping buffer heat stress under high temperatures (Shishegar, 2014; Dong et al., 2024). However, UGS does not provide same levels of cooling benefits. The cooling-effectiveness of UGS is heterogeneous, exhibiting spatial variation depending on factors such as size, vegetation density, shape, and spatial configuration (Wang and Ren, 2025). Consequently, spatial proximity to green space does not equate to access to thermal comfort. As cooling performance varies, the extent of its true benefits depends on reachability shaped by temporal, functional and normative factors such as travel time, mode, cost, and duration (Miller, 2018; Hassan et al., 2025). Despite growing recognition of UGS as a cooling infrastructure, both its distribution and cooling benefits remain uneven across neighbourhoods, particularly among lower socioeconomic and ethnic minority groups (Harlan et al., 2006; Li et al., 2026). Such inequalities demonstrate that urban heat mitigation is not only an environmental concern, but also a growing socio-spatial challenge. All things considered, green space provision reflects wider patterns of environmental injustice, and access to cooling relief is shaped by structural urban inequalities rather than by heat exposure alone (Wolch, Byrne and Newell, 2014).

Taking London as a case study, the city has experienced severe and escalating heat events but remains unprepared for its consequences (Mayor of London, 2026b). Notably, London recorded 59 days above 30°C between 2014 and 2023, and 5 days above 35°C between 2019 and 2022 (IIED, n.d.). The Mayor of London (n.d.) noted that existing homes and buildings in London are not built for the warming climate, with projected impacts on health and social care systems set to increase. Many of those heat related fatalities are considered preventable, yet the London Climate Resilience Review argues that England is currently “ill-prepared” for heat risk with limited cooling opportunities, calling for urgent attention and action (Boyd, Leigh and Sutton, 2024). In response, the London Plan 2021 identifies urban green infrastructure as a key mechanism for addressing UHI, promoting health and wellbeing, and improving accessibility of open spaces (Mayor of London, 2021). Despite strong policy commitments, spatial evidence on whether London’s green infrastructure provides accessible cooling relief to neighbourhoods most exposed to heat and socioeconomic deprivation remains limited. By applying data driven insights across heat vulnerable areas, cooling-effectiveness, and multimodal accessibility, this allows for informing more equitable urban greening strategies.

While existing literature shows the ability of UGS to mitigate urban heat and acknowledges its variation in cooling performance, few studies have integrated this into fields of cooling-effectiveness, accessibility, and environmental equity within one research framework. Consequently, based on existing research gaps, this study therefore aims to address 12 the research question of: To what extent is multimodal accessibility to cooling-effective urban green spaces equitably distributed across heat exposed and socioeconomically deprived areas in London? The specific objectives include:

  1. Through Earth observation indicators, which urban green space demonstrates cooling benefits, and which neighbourhoods experience greatest heat exposure in London?

  2. How accessible are cooling-effective green spaces across London’s neighbourhoods considering multi-modal transportation?

  3. To what extent do heat exposed and socioeconomically deprived neighbourhoods experience differentiated access to cooling-effective green spaces?
